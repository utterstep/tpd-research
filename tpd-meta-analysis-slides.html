(When) do teacher professional development interventions improve student achievement?

A meta-analysis of 128 high-quality studies and 356 effect sizes spanning 31 years of in-service TPD research (1990–2021).
Adrie J. Visscher · Natasha Dmoshinskaia · Marta Pellegrini · Anny Rey-Naizaque
University of Twente (NL) · University of Cagliari (IT)
doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100742 · CC BY · open access
01Why this question matters

Billions spent — but does it reach students?

  • Teacher quality explains ~10–15% of variance in student achievement — teachers matter, and skills plateau after 5–10 years.
  • In-service TPD is the main lever to keep improving teaching at scale.
  • Spending is enormous — the U.S. alone spent ~$18 billion/year on TPD (2014 estimate), likely more today.
  • The recurring question: does that investment pay off in student achievement?

02Research questions

Three questions

RQ1

How big is the effect?

What is the average effect of in-service TPD on students' academic achievement?

RQ2

Does the goal matter?

What is the average effect per TPD goal (e.g. content knowledge, curricula, digital tools)?

RQ3

What conditions help?

What factors moderate the effect — study design, context, and TPD design features?

A distinctive angle: the team tested four learning-theory principles as moderators — something prior TPD reviews had barely examined.

Section 1 · Introduction02 / 14
03Design — built to minimize bias

Strict inclusion criteria for high-quality evidence

  • RCTs & quasi-experiments only, with business-as-usual control groups.
  • Independent outcome measures — researcher/developer-made tests excluded.
  • Pre-K to grade 12, regular schools; ≥20 teachers/classes per condition.
  • Duration ≥12 weeks; TPD delivered by teachers (not researchers in class).
  • Baseline equivalence (<0.25 SD); differential attrition <15%.
  • Published 1990–2021 — 31 years, broader than any prior review.

A distinctive verification step

The team conducted 115 interviews with researchers & TPD facilitators worldwide — covering 90% of included publications — to verify and complete details that publications left vague.

Analysis: Hedges' g, random-effects, correlated-effects model with robust variance estimation; coded with the MUTOS framework.

05What was tested as a moderator

Six moderators — and four learning-theory principles

Four learning-theory principles

Forms of support for teachers learning complex skills:

  • Teacher performance standards — clear definition of "good" teaching
  • Teacher self-regulation — monitoring & planning one's own learning
  • Teacher coaching — feedback in the classroom
  • Teacher cooperation — mutual exchange among teachers

Plus study & design features

  • Number of TPD hours
  • Trainer type — external vs. researcher/developer
  • TPD goal — (P)CK, comprehensive approaches, curricula, digital tools, other
  • Grade level, SES, tested subject, publication status

Key idea: the principles were studied as a set, not in isolation — combinations may matter more than any single one.

06Main finding · RQ1

On average, TPD works — a medium effect

+0.09
average weighted effect size (Hedges' g)
95% CI [0.07, 0.11] · p < .001
  • By Kraft's (2020/2023) benchmarks for standardized tests, 0.09 counts as a medium-size effect on achievement.
  • Consistent with the most rigorous recent reviews (Sims et al. 2023: 0.05; Fletcher-Wood & Zuccollo 2020: 0.09).
  • Even slightly higher than comparable reviews despite stricter inclusion criteria.
  • At scale, a 0.09 effect across millions of students is meaningful.

"TPD activities are not a goal in themselves" — the point is improved teaching and, ultimately, student learning.

  • Trainer type — external trainers = developers/researchers

Trainer finding is good news for scaling: external trainers (always needed at scale) work as well as the program's own developers.

11Uncertainties & limitations

Read with these caveats

  • Geography: 95% of studies from the US/UK — generalization elsewhere is unknown.
  • Coverage skew: 67% primary, 65% reading — little evidence for other levels and subjects.
  • Missing SES: 28% of studies didn't report SES; assumed average — may mask a real SES effect.
  • Correlation ≠ cause: moderator results describe relationships, not causal effects.
  • Short horizon: effects measured right after TPD; only 4 studies ran 3 years. Long-run impact is unknown.
  • Thin reporting: fidelity of implementation reported in only ~12% of studies; the "black box" (Desimone blocks 2 & 3) stays closed.
  • Averages hide subgroups: a near-zero average may still hide strong gains (or harms) for specific teacher/student groups.
  • Publication bias present despite efforts to include gray literature.
Section 4.5 · Limitations11 / 14
12Implications for practice

What designers & school leaders can take away

Expect modest, real gains

On average TPD delivers a positive, medium effect — but with no guarantees. Plan for variability.

Build in learning-theory support

Combine performance standards, self-regulation, coaching & cooperation — more principles trended toward more impact.

Don't over-invest in hours

Simply adding TPD hours didn't raise effectiveness. Design quality beats sheer quantity.

Scaling is feasible

External trainers were as effective as program developers — encouraging for rollout at scale.

Strongest bet: primary grades

Effects were largest for primary education; weaker for mixed-grade programs.

For researchers

Report fidelity, evidence base, SES, and publish null results to enable better future syntheses.

Section 4.6 · Implications for practice12 / 14
13In one slide

Key takeaways

  • TPD works on average: +0.09 (medium effect on achievement).
  • But it varies wildly (−0.15 to +0.32); ~15% of effects are null/negative.
  • The goal of TPD and the number of hours didn't predict success.
  • Applying more learning-theory principles trended toward bigger impact.
  • Larger effects in primary grades; external trainers work fine for scaling.

The field has answered "does TPD work?"

The frontier is now "why do some programs work and others don't?"

A learning-theory lens — and better reporting of what actually happens inside TPD — is the way forward.
